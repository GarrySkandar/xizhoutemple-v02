# Mountain Gate

Chinese Buddhist monasteries often use the Mountain Gate as the starting point of the central axis. Ancient monasteries were usually built in mountains and forests, hence the name Mountain Gate. The Mountain Gate is not merely the architectural entrance; it also carries ritual and symbolic meaning, reminding visitors to gather body and mind as they enter a pure and solemn space of learning and practice.

## Architectural Form
The Mountain Gate follows the symmetrical regulations of traditional Chinese architecture. It often has a single-eaved xieshan roof, blue-gray tiles, vermilion walls, upturned eaves, and painted bracket sets, giving it a solemn and elegant appearance. There are two mainstream forms:
**Hall-style Mountain Gate:** Three gate openings are joined into one hall through which visitors may pass. Guardian deity statues are installed inside the hall. This is the most common form in monasteries of the Central Plains and Jiangnan regions.
**Paifang-style Mountain Gate:** An independent stone or wooden ceremonial archway, with three separate openings and no hall walls. It is often placed at the foot of the mountain or outside the monastery, creating spatial distance between the secular world and the place of practice.
**Orderly three-gate arrangement:** The central opening is the widest and tallest, while the two side openings are slightly smaller. Stone steps and splayed screen walls are often placed before the gate to heighten solemn dignity. The lintel carries the monastery name plaque, and Chan-style couplets are placed on both sides, pointing to the original mind of practice.

## Buddhist Meaning of the Three Gates: The Three Gates of Liberation
The three openings are not merely passageways. They correspond to the core Buddhist meanings of liberation and are a typical example of Chan meaning hidden within architectural form:
**Center · Gate of Emptiness:** The largest gate, symbolizing liberation through emptiness. It means seeing through all things as arising from conditions and being empty of inherent existence, without clinging to them as truly real. The common expression “entering the gate of emptiness” refers to entering monastic life through this gate and cutting off worldly entanglements. Ordinary lay devotees usually do not pass through the central gate when going to worship, as an expression of humility and respect.
**East Side · Gate of Signlessness:** Symbolizes liberation through signlessness. It means not clinging to outward forms, beauty and ugliness, gain and loss, and letting go of attachment to appearances.
**West Side · Gate of Wishlessness:** Symbolizes liberation through non-action or wishlessness. It means cutting off the fabrications of greed, anger, and delusion, no longer producing new afflictions, and abiding in the pure original mind.
Passing through the three gates signifies putting down worldly disturbances, moving from the ordinary toward the sacred, and beginning the path of mind cultivation.

## Guardians Inside the Hall: Vajra Warriors, the Heng and Ha Generals
Inside the hall, two tall guardian Vajra figures stand on the left and right. They are the dedicated protectors of the Mountain Gate and are iconic images in Chinese Buddhist monasteries:
In Buddhist origins, they are known as Secret-Traces Vajra and Nārāyaṇa Deva. Holding vajra pestles and appearing wrathful and majestic, they subdue demons and protect the Buddhist monastery.
Under the influence of the popular novel Fengshen Yanyi, folk tradition commonly calls them the “Heng and Ha Generals”: the one with closed mouth and tightened nose is General Heng, Zheng Lun, whose nose emits white vapor; the one with open mouth and exposed teeth is General Ha, Chen Qi, whose mouth emits yellow vapor. With one “heng” and one “ha,” they intimidate false and scattered thoughts.
The two statues, one still and one active, convey beneath their majestic appearance the teaching that people should subdue the demons of the mind and guard the pure mind of practice.

## Spatial and Ritual Meaning
The Mountain Gate is the first boundary of the monastery. One wall and three gates separate the noise of the red dust from the purity of the Buddhist sanctuary. Upon entering, visitors naturally restrain restlessness and give rise to reverence. It is both the beginning of the architectural sequence and the beginning of inner cultivation. Through a visible gate, it carries the invisible Dharma principles of liberation, letting go, and guarding the mind into every visitor’s first step into the monastery.
