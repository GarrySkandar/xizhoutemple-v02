# Drum Tower

The Drum Tower is located on the west side of the Hall of Heavenly Kings, forming an east-west pair with the Bell Tower on the east side. Its form, height, and architectural specifications are completely parallel to the Bell Tower. Built as a two-story pavilion, it is solemn and harmonious in appearance, maintaining the Chinese ritual layout of left-right balance along the temple’s central axis.

## Buddhist Symbolism of the Great Drum
**Proclaiming the wondrous Dharma, its sound shaking the ten directions:** The drum’s sound is deep and powerful, like the Buddha proclaiming the Dharma, resounding through the four directions so that all beings may hear the true teaching and leave wrong views behind. The bell awakens; the drum proclaims. One is still, one is active, and the two complement each other.
**Subduing demonic obstacles and stabilizing the monastery:** The drum has a majestic and awe-inspiring force. It can subdue heterodox demons and the inner demons of scattered thoughts, protecting the purity and peace of the monastery so that afflictions do not disturb those who practice.
**Encouraging the mind of diligence:** The drum’s sound is vigorous and uplifting, urging monks and lay devotees to practice diligently, without laziness or retreat, advancing courageously toward goodness and cultivating blessings and wisdom.


## Temple Drum-Ringing Rituals and Functions
**Morning, during the Yin hour, 3:00–5:00 a.m.:** The bell is struck first, then the drum is beaten. The morning bell usually begins quickly and then slows, intended to wake people from sleep. When the bell fades, the drum begins.
**Evening, during the Xu hour, 5:00–7:00 p.m.:** The drum is beaten first, then the bell is struck. The evening drum usually begins slowly and then quickens, signaling the end of the day and preparing people for sleep or curfew. When the drum fades, the bell follows.
**Historical note:** From the Han dynasty to the early Tang dynasty, the order was actually the reverse, known as “morning drum and evening bell.” Cai Yong’s Duduan of the Han dynasty says: “The drum moves the multitude; the bell stops the multitude. When the night watches are exhausted, the drum sounds and people rise; when the day watches are exhausted, the bell sounds and people rest.” At that time, the drum’s stirring sound was considered suitable for urging people to rise and work, while the calm sound of the bell was considered suitable for rest. After the middle Tang dynasty, this gradually evolved into the system we now know as “morning bell and evening drum.”
**Calling the assembly:** During morning and evening services, formal meals, precept recitations, and Dharma assemblies, the drum is used as a signal to notify the entire monastic community to gather in the main hall and practice the Dharma together.
**Expressing solemnity in Dharma assemblies:** During large blessing ceremonies, ordination ceremonies, and Water-Land Dharma assemblies, the bell and drum are sounded alternately to heighten the solemn atmosphere of the monastery and support the complete merit of the Dharma assembly.


## Summary of the Combined Meaning of Bell and Drum
Bell on the left and drum on the right, east and west, together form the temple’s complete system of sound teaching:
The bell corresponds to compassion that removes suffering and liberates beings sinking in the six realms; the drum corresponds to Dharma protection and diligence, sustaining the true Dharma so that it may remain long in the world.
Morning bell and evening drum accompany the day from dawn to dusk, reminding all who come and go: worldly life is like a dream; one should diligently cultivate wholesome Dharma and swiftly realize bodhi.
The morning bell dispels dullness and obscurity; the evening drum gathers the scattered mind of the day, reminding everyone to collect the mind, reflect inwardly, put down worldly concerns, and practice in calmness. When bell and drum sound in the morning and evening, they symbolize practice that continues day and night.
