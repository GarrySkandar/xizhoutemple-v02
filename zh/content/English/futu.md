# Seven-Tier Stupa

A common saying goes: “Saving one life is better than building a seven-tier stupa.” “Stupa” is the Sanskrit term rendered into Chinese as “futu,” meaning a pagoda or reliquary tower. In its earliest form, a stupa was built to enshrine the Buddha’s relics, scriptures, or the remains of eminent monks.


## The True Teaching Meaning of the “Seven-Tier Stupa”

“Seven” does not merely indicate a number; it signifies completeness: In Buddhist teaching, “seven” represents fullness or perfection, such as the four directions, above and below, plus the center. It symbolizes turning delusion into awakening and the completeness of one’s own nature.
The progressive ascent of the seven tiers, countering greed, anger, and delusion: Building a pagoda from the first tier to the seventh symbolizes that Buddhist practice must remove attachments layer by layer and continually elevate one’s spiritual state. In Mahayana Buddhism, the “seven tiers” are often associated with the Seven Factors of Awakening: investigation of the Dharma, diligence, joy, pliancy, mindfulness, concentration, and equanimity.
Building a stupa and seeing a stupa are meant to remind us: cultivating virtue is like building a pagoda. The foundation must be firm, and one must continually elevate one’s spiritual realm until reaching the great completeness symbolized by the seventh tier.
The true meaning of saving one life: Saving a person’s physical life is an act of rescue, while the Buddhadharma saves a person’s “Dharma-body wisdom-life.” To build a pagoda so that passersby may see it and plant wholesome causes rooted in the Dharma, or to help someone break through delusion, awaken to truth, abandon evil, and cultivate goodness through teaching the Dharma, is to “build the seven-tier stupa within one’s own nature.” Its merit is immeasurable.

## The Merit of Circumambulating a Stupa: Raising Mindfulness in Motion
At major sacred sites, devotees can often be seen walking and bowing around stupas again and again. Buddhist scriptures say that “circumambulating three times brings immeasurable merit.”

**Why must one circumambulate to the right, that is, clockwise?**
Clockwise circumambulation was the highest form of respect in ancient India. To walk clockwise around a stupa symbolizes living in society with accordance, adaptability, and tolerance, letting go of arrogance and attachment so as to support and fulfill others.

**How should one train the mind through circumstances while circumambulating a stupa?**
When walking around a stupa, one absolutely should not let the mind wander or chat casually while walking. Doing so may at most strengthen the body, but it does not produce true Buddhist merit. True merit comes from purity of the three karmas:
Body circumambulates the stupa: The body is moving, the steps are steady, neither hurried nor agitated. This cultivates purity of bodily karma.
Mouth recites the Buddha’s name or a mantra: The mouth sincerely recites “Amitabha Buddha,” without idle talk. This cultivates purity of verbal karma.
Mind contemplates: The stupa stands unmoving at the center, representing the “true mind as the fundamental essence.” We revolve around it at the outside, representing the changing affairs and phenomena of the world. We should learn from the stupa: no matter how the outer world turns or how life rises and falls, the mind remains settled on Amitabha Buddha, without giving rise to wandering thoughts or being shaken. This cultivates purity of mental karma.

## Summary of Practice
We circumambulate a stupa not to seek some power from stone or clay inside it to bless us with wealth. If, after walking one circuit, the greed, anger, and delusion in your mind decrease by one measure, that is called merit. If you walk ten circuits yet still have the same bad temper and go home to quarrel with your wife or husband, then you have walked in vain. That is only stretching your muscles.
When seeing a seven-tier stupa, reflect: are my thoughts and intentions moving upward, toward the Seven Factors of Awakening?
When circumambulating the stupa clockwise, regard the stupa as your own true mind. Let the wind and rain outside turn however they may; your mind should remain as steady as the stupa. Bring this “power of concentration” back to your family and your workplace. In the world of dust, no matter how others tempt or provoke you, there is only one clear phrase in your mind: “Amitabha Buddha,” steady as Mount Tai. This is the true merit of circumambulating a stupa.
